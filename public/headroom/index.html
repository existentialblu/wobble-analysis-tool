Headroom

Where an ASV spends its pressure support range, one slice per night.

What this shows

An ASV raises and lowers its pressure support (PS) all night to keep your breathing steady. Headroom shows how it went about that each night: how much of its range it used, how fast it moved, and how often it ran into its limits. It's experimental and not medical advice.

  1. Load your SD card's DATALOG folder, or some PLD files. Nothing is uploaded.
  2. Move along the track with scroll, drag, the arrow keys or Play. Each slice is one night.
  3. Mark the first night after something changed, such as settings or a medication, then switch to Difference from baseline to see what moved.

Data

Example data, invented

Headroom reads ResMed *_PLD.edf files and keeps only ASV sessions (fixed pressure support is skipped), plus STR.edf for the settings in force each night if you have it. Other files in the folder are skipped without being read. Each batch you add joins the nights already loaded, so a phone can go one day folder at a time. On a computer, pick or drop the whole SD card. Nothing leaves this browser.

    How to read it

    The cloud

    Each slice is one night. Left to right is pressure support (PS); up and down is how fast PS was moving: above the middle line it was rising, below it falling. Darker means more of the night was spent there, from the machine's 2-second samples.

    The orange lines

    The night's PS limits: the floor (minimum) and the ceiling (maximum). They get thicker the more of the night PS sat on them. Joined from slice to slice they form rails, so a settings change shows as a step.

    The kite

    A four-corner summary of each night. Left and right are where PS usually sat low and high (its 10th and 90th percentiles). Top is how fast it typically climbed and bottom how fast it typically fell (90th percentiles of the rate of change), each placed at the average PS while climbing or falling. In the difference view, the dashed kite is the baseline era's.

    We call it a kite. Strictly it's a four-corner glyph; it only becomes a geometric kite on nights when rises and falls mirror each other, which makes the lopsidedness the interesting part.

    Rise ÷ fall

    The kite's rise speed divided by its fall speed. Above 1, PS climbed faster than it backed off; below 1, it climbed more gently than it backed off. In two years of one person's data on two ResMed machines, it stayed close to 0.9 through settings changes, a firmware change and a medication change, so it may mostly reflect the machine's algorithm rather than the person. Whether it differs between people or machines is an open question.

    Common patterns

    A calm night is a small smudge near the floor. Pumping stretches into a wide loop through the middle. Bouncing between the limits piles up dark columns on both orange lines.
